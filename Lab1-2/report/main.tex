\begin{abstract}
This report presents a simulator for a differential-drive mobile robot and a
dead-reckoning localization method for it. Planar direct and inverse pose
compounding are implemented in the Pre-Lab and reused by both the simulator
and the estimator. Lab 1 completes the motion model and the simulated encoder
and compass readings. Lab 2 converts the encoder readings into body motion and
integrates it to estimate the robot pose. A circular and a figure-eight
trajectory are tested, and four velocity gains and four encoder-noise levels
are compared. The gain barely changes the result, while more encoder noise
produces more drift.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Pre-Lab: Planar pose operations}
\label{sec:prelab}

\subsubsection{Planar pose representation}

A three-degree-of-freedom pose is
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\eta}=
    \begin{bmatrix}x&y&\psi\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\R^{3},
\end{equation}
where $(x,y)$ is the position and $\psi$ the yaw angle. Its equivalent
homogeneous transformation is
\begin{equation}
    T(\boldsymbol{\eta})=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \cos\psi & -\sin\psi & x\\
        \sin\psi &  \cos\psi & y\\
        0 & 0 & 1
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:pose-transform}
\end{equation}
Direct compounding corresponds to multiplying two of these matrices, and
inverse compounding to inverting one.

\subsubsection{Direct pose compounding}

Let ${}^{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{B}$ describe frame $B$ relative to frame $A$, and
${}^{B}\boldsymbol{x}_{C}$ frame $C$ relative to $B$. The pose of $C$
relative to $A$ is
\begin{equation}
    {}^{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{C}
    ={}^{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{B}\opluspose{}^{B}\boldsymbol{x}_{C},
\end{equation}
which is equivalent to
$T({}^{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{C})=T({}^{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{B})
T({}^{B}\boldsymbol{x}_{C})$. In coordinate form~\cite[p.~24]{slidesIntro},
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
{}^{A}x_C\\{}^{A}y_C\\{}^{A}\psi_C
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
{}^{A}x_B+{}^{B}x_C\cos({}^{A}\psi_B)-{}^{B}y_C\sin({}^{A}\psi_B)\\
{}^{A}y_B+{}^{B}x_C\sin({}^{A}\psi_B)+{}^{B}y_C\cos({}^{A}\psi_B)\\
{}^{A}\psi_B+{}^{B}\psi_C
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:oplus}
\end{equation}

% [CAMBIO] Parrafo del "por que oplus" compactado, misma idea.
$\oplus$ is needed because ${}^{B}\boldsymbol{x}_{C}$ is written in frame $B$,
so it cannot be added directly to ${}^{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{B}$. The operator first
rotates it into frame $A$ and then adds both poses. The code evaluates
\cref{eq:oplus} and returns a new \texttt{Pose3D}:
\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Core of the direct compounding implementation.}]
x_result = xB + xC * cos(theta_B) - yC * sin(theta_B)
y_result = yB + xC * sin(theta_B) + yC * cos(theta_B)
theta_result = theta_B + theta_C
return Pose3D(np.array([[x_result], [y_result], [theta_result]]))
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Inverse pose compounding}

Inverse compounding gives frame $A$ relative to frame $B$ from the pose of
$B$ relative to $A$~\cite[p.~27]{slidesIntro}:
\begin{equation}
{}^{B}\boldsymbol{x}_{A}=\ominuspose{}^{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{B}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
-{}^{A}x_B\cos({}^{A}\psi_B)-{}^{A}y_B\sin({}^{A}\psi_B)\\
 {}^{A}x_B\sin({}^{A}\psi_B)-{}^{A}y_B\cos({}^{A}\psi_B)\\
-{}^{A}\psi_B
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:ominus}
\end{equation}
which is the coordinate form of
$T({}^{B}\boldsymbol{x}_{A})=T({}^{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{B})^{-1}$. The code
evaluates \cref{eq:ominus} directly:
\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Core of the inverse compounding implementation.}]
x_result = -xB * cos(theta_B) - yB * sin(theta_B)
y_result =  xB * sin(theta_B) - yB * cos(theta_B)
theta_result = -theta_B
return Pose3D(np.array([[x_result], [y_result], [theta_result]]))
\end{lstlisting}

Both operators were tested in the Pre-Lab notebook and then copied into
\texttt{Pose3D.py}, so the simulator and the estimator use them as
\texttt{Pose3D} methods.

\begin{equation}

\begin{equation}

\end{equation}

\subsection{\texttt{fs}: motion model}

% [CAMBIO] Quitada la figura fs_motion_model_slide.png (captura de la
% diapositiva). Motivos: es el modelo del AUV de 4 DOF (con z, w y k4, que

\texttt{fs} computes the new simulated state from the previous one and the
commanded forward and angular velocities, $\boldsymbol{u}_{s,k}=[u_d,\,r_d]^{T}$.
The wheels prevent sideways motion, so the desired body velocity is
$\boldsymbol{\nu}_d=[u_d,\,0,\,r_d]^{T}$. Following the slides
model~\cite[p.~33]{slidesIntro} for the planar robot, the motion model is
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{x}_{s,k}=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \boldsymbol{\eta}_{s,k-1}\opluspose\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{s,k-1}\Delta t
        +\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{w}_{s,k}\Delta t^{2}\right)\\
        \boldsymbol{\nu}_{s,k-1}+K(\boldsymbol{\nu}_d-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{s,k-1})
        +\boldsymbol{w}_{s,k}\Delta t
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad \boldsymbol{w}_{s,k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},Q_s).
    \label{eq:fs}
\end{equation}
The first row moves the pose with the previous body velocity plus the
displacement caused by a random acceleration. $\oplus$ rotates this
body-frame increment into the world frame. The second row drives the velocity
towards $\boldsymbol{\nu}_d$ through the diagonal gain
$K=\operatorname{diag}(k_1,k_2,k_3)$, $k_i>0$. The implementation uses
$K=I_3$; \cref{sec:results} discusses this choice. The key lines of \texttt{fs} are:
\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Pose and velocity update in the simulator.}]
w = np.random.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(3), self.Qsk).reshape(3, 1)
eta_new = eta_prev.oplus(Pose3D(
    nu_prev * self.dt + 0.5 * w * self.dt**2))
nu_new = nu_prev + K @ (nu_desired - nu_prev) + w * self.dt
\end{lstlisting}

Step 1 needs $\ominus$ because the simulator stores both poses in the world
frame, while an encoder measures the displacement in the robot frame. In
transformation form,
\begin{equation}
    T(\Delta\boldsymbol{\eta}_k)=
    T(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{s,k-1})^{-1}
    T(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{s,k}).
\end{equation}
$\ominus$ removes the previous world-frame pose, and $\oplus$ then expresses
the current pose relative to the previous robot frame. A plain subtraction
would not rotate the displacement into the body axes, and the wheel motion
would come out wrong whenever the robot is not aligned with the world frame.
The method returns $(\boldsymbol{z}_{s,k},R_e)$, where
$\boldsymbol{z}_{s,k}=[n_L,n_R]^T$ holds the noisy pulses of each wheel.

\subsection{Dead-reckoning pose update}

The estimator integrates the encoder-derived velocity over one sampling period
and compounds that body-frame displacement with the previous
estimate~\cite[p.~37]{slidesIntro}:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_k
    =\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k-1}
    \opluspose
    \left(\boldsymbol{\nu}_k\Delta t\right).
    \label{eq:dead-reckoning}
\end{equation}
Multiplying by $\Delta t$ turns the velocity into a local pose increment, and
$\oplus$ rotates it with the previous heading before adding it to the
world-frame pose. The full method is:
\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Complete \texttt{Localize} implementation.}]
def Localize(self, xk_1, uk):
    self.etak_1 = xk_1
    self.uk = uk
    nu_k = uk

    etak = Pose3D(xk_1).oplus(nu_k * self.dt)
    return etak
\end{lstlisting}

% [CAMBIO] Ampliado este parrafo. Antes solo decia que los errores se
% integran. Ahora explica el mecanismo de la deriva (el error de rumbo gira
% todos los desplazamientos siguientes) y que solo propagamos una pose, sin
% incertidumbre (R_e se descarta). Es la idea principal del Lab 2 y enlaza

No compass, landmark or absolute-position measurement enters this update.
Each encoder error goes into the next pose and nothing corrects it. Heading
errors hurt the most: a wrong yaw rotates every later displacement, so the
position error grows with the distance travelled. In addition, only a single
pose is propagated: \texttt{GetInput} discards the covariance $R_e$ returned by
\texttt{ReadEncoders}, so the estimator does not quantify how this
uncertainty grows.

\subsection{Effect of the velocity gain}

% [CAMBIO] Añadido que con k >= 2 diverge (antes decia que cualquier
% k > 1 "overshoots before settling", que solo vale para 1 < k < 2).
% Añadido el por que de que las estimaciones salgan iguales: K solo
% afecta al simulador, el estimador no lo usa.
$K$ sets how fast the simulated velocity follows the command through
$K(\boldsymbol{\nu}_d-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{s,k-1})$. With $K=kI_3$, $k<1$
applies only part of the correction at each step, $k=1$ applies all of it in
one step, and $1<k<2$ overshoots before settling ($k\geq2$ would diverge). The
values $k=0.25$, $0.5$, $1$ and $1.5$ are tested with the nominal encoder noise
of 22 pulses (\cref{fig:gain-trajectories}).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{gain_trajectory_comparison.png}
    \caption{Simulated (orange) and dead-reckoning (blue) trajectories for the
    four tested velocity gains. Top: first turn. Bottom: figure-eight.}
    \label{fig:gain-trajectories}
\end{figure}

All four gains give almost the same trajectories, since the velocity
transient lasts a few steps out of 2094. The estimator never uses $K$: the
gain only changes the simulated (true) path, so the dead-reckoning estimates
also look the same. $K=I_3$ is kept as the simplest option.

and 44 pulses, where $\sigma_e$ is the standard deviation of the encoder noise.
The nominal 22 pulses comes from $R_e$; it is a noise level, unrelated to the
resolution $N_p$.

% que tenemos: el simulador sigue metiendo ruido de proceso Q_s y aun asi

With $\sigma_e=0$ the estimate lies on top of the true path, even though the
simulator still applies the process noise $Q_s$. The encoders measure the
wheel motion that really happened, so process noise changes the true path but
adds no estimation error. With 11 pulses a small gap appears, at 22 it is
clear, and at 44 the estimate gets badly deformed. For dead reckoning, the
encoder noise matters much more than $K$.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

% [CAMBIO] Quitado "verify the expected robot motion": no medimos nada que
% lo verifique. Ahora resume la cadena completa y la limitacion principal.
The two labs build a complete chain from planar pose compounding to simulated
sensing and odometric localization. \texttt{Pose3D} provides the $\oplus$ and
$\ominus$ operators that both the simulator and the estimator reuse. The
simulator generates the true motion with process noise and the matching noisy
encoder pulses, and the dead-reckoning class inverts the encoder model to
integrate the pose. The circular and figure-eight tests show the expected
trajectories and the main limitation of dead reckoning: without an absolute
observation, encoder errors accumulate and the drift grows with the distance
travelled.
